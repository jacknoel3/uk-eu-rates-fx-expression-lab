## 1. Forward and Spot Exchange Rates

**Author and edition:** Eugene F. Fama; *Journal of Monetary Economics* 14 (1984), 319–338. **Local file:** `forward and spot exchange rates (fama 1984).pdf`. **Read:** all 20 pages, including seven tables and ten numbered equations; the scan's defective text layer was checked against every page image. [Publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304393284900461).

**Question.** Does the forward–spot differential predict subsequent currency movements, and what does that relationship reveal about expected depreciation and time-varying premiums?

**Data and method.** Thirty-day forwards and spot quotes for nine currencies against USD, sampled at Friday closes every four weeks: 122 observations from 31 August 1973 to 10 December 1982. Quotes are USD per unit of foreign currency; the analysis uses logs, with differences reported in percent. Fama estimates complementary regressions of the subsequent spot change and the forward-minus-subsequent-spot difference on today's forward–spot differential. He then uses seemingly unrelated regressions (SUR) to account for correlated currency outcomes and repeats the analysis in two 61-observation subsamples (pp. 2–5, 7–16).

**Central decomposition.** In the paper, `F` and `S` denote **log** forward and spot prices, and expectations use information available at entry time `t`:

```text
F_t - S_t = P_t + E_t[S_T - S_t]
P_t       = F_t - E_t[S_T]
```

The forward premium therefore combines an expected spot change and a premium. This initially defines the premium; interpreting it economically requires a model. Covered interest parity links matched-tenor forward prices to interest differentials under the paper's no-arbitrage assumptions. It does not require the forward to be an unbiased forecast of future spot. PPP and Fisher-equation arguments are illustrative explanations, not assumptions imposed throughout the empirical analysis (pp. 2–5).

**Findings and important details.**

- **Predictability is small relative to realised volatility.** Forward premiums are persistent, while observed spot changes show little autocorrelation. Most of the spot movement is unexpected; a non-zero regression coefficient does not imply accurate point forecasts or large explained variance (pp. 5–7).
- **The forward-premium puzzle appears clearly.** All full-sample OLS slopes for subsequent spot changes are negative rather than the unit slope associated with an unbiased forward forecast. Individually, most negative slopes are imprecise; the joint analysis provides stronger evidence. A common-slope SUR estimate is −0.58, with standard error 0.13 (Tables 2 and 4, pp. 8, 12).
- **The rational-expectations interpretation is conditional.** Negative spot-change slopes imply negative covariance between the premium and expected spot change, and larger premium variance than expected-change variance. Covariance can offset the components, so the slopes are not simple variance shares and cannot separately identify both variances (pp. 3, 9).
- **The two regressions are one accounting identity.** Their slopes sum to one, intercepts to zero, and residuals cancel period by period. They illuminate the decomposition but do not provide independent confirmations. Subperiod joint tests retain evidence of time variation, while rejecting equal slopes across currencies: the pooled −0.58 is not a universal law (pp. 3, 13–16).
- **The explanation is unresolved.** Fama discusses inefficient expectations, currency intervention, rare anticipated events absent from the realised sample, and international real-return/PPP mechanisms. The regressions do not conclusively select among these stories (pp. 16–19).

**Direct implication for the lab.** Keep spot movement, forward/carry contribution and costs separate, and retain the carry-only benchmark. A forward premium is not a pure policy forecast or evidence of policy alpha. For the lab's GBP-per-EUR quote, a positive EUR notional `N_EUR` sold forward at the quote `K_t,T`, locked at entry `t`, has settlement cash P&L `N_EUR × (K_t,T − Q_T)` in GBP, where `Q_T` is EUR/GBP spot at the original settlement date `T`. Sterling strengthening increases this payoff; it is positive only when `Q_T < K_t,T`, since negative carry can offset a spot gain. The corresponding diagnostic log expression is `log(K_t,T) − log(Q_T)`, decomposable into forward premium minus EUR/GBP spot change; it is not an exact return on capital. This sign translation is our application, not a trading result estimated in the paper.

**Limitations and implementation status.** The historical USD panel predates the euro and does not establish the EUR/GBP relation, causal policy exposure or modern net profitability. Thirty-day quotes are compared with four-week spot changes, not exact contractual settlement valuations. Log expectations also differ from expected cash payoffs. The weekly reset must still mark the old forward to its original settlement date under FROZEN D028; the settlement illustration above cannot replace that engine. No new signal, premium estimator or instrument approval is introduced.

## 2. Currency Momentum Strategies

**Authors and edition:** Lukas Menkhoff, Lucio Sarno, Maik Schmeling and Andreas Schrimpf; extended manuscript associated with *Journal of Financial Economics* 106(3) (2012), 660–684. The repository cover labels the version unspecified; the title page says a shorter version is forthcoming in the journal. **Local file:** `currency momentum strategies (menkhoff 2012).pdf`. **Read:** all 88 pages, including the embedded appendix, 28 tables and nine figures. [Repository record for this copy](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/3296/).

**Question.** Do recent currency winners continue to outperform losers, how does this differ from carry or technical trading, and what prevents the apparent profits from being easily arbitraged?

**Data and method.** Month-end spot and one-month forward quotes cover January 1976–January 2010, with 48 currencies appearing in an unbalanced panel. Coverage changes substantially; these are not 48 continuously tradable currencies. Each month, currencies are sorted into six equally weighted portfolios using past excess returns over 1/3/6/9/12 months, with corresponding holding-period alternatives. Buying winners and selling losers removes the common USD long exposure. Quotes are foreign-currency units per USD, the inverse of Fama's convention: foreign-currency excess returns are approximated by the forward discount minus the subsequent log spot change. Separate spot-momentum portfolios sort on past spot changes (pp. 11–16).

**Findings and important details.**

- **Strong gross momentum, sensitive to horizon.** A one-month formation/one-month holding strategy earns 9.46% annualised, with Sharpe 0.95; six-month formation earns 8.54%, with Sharpe 0.79. Longer holding periods generally weaken performance. Spot momentum also exists, so the effect is not simply accumulated carry. However, the excess-return and spot columns use different sorts: subtracting their means is not an exact carry attribution (Table 1, p. 51).
- **Momentum and carry are distinct.** Winner-minus-loser momentum correlations with carry are close to zero; double sorts and regressions retain momentum after controlling for forward discounts. Cross-sectional momentum also differs from individual-currency moving-average rules. These findings do not establish that momentum reliably hedges carry during crises (pp. 21–27; Tables 3–7, pp. 53–57).
- **Costs consume much of the headline return.** The one-month strategy falls to 3.92% net, with a t-statistic of 2.20; twelve-month formation falls to 1.40%, statistically insignificant. Monthly turnover exceeds 70% for both winners and losers, and those currencies have wider spreads than the average. Every monthly forward roll crosses its relevant bid/ask; spot costs depend on entry and exit. The authors regard indicative quoted spreads as conservative relative to effective spreads, an assumption rather than evidence from actual fills (pp. 29–31; Tables 8–9, pp. 58–59; Table A.4, p. 76).
- **The currency universe changes the conclusion.** Among 15 developed-country currencies, one-month momentum earns 3.83% gross and only 0.79% net, with t-statistic 0.44. Its post-1992 developed-country net mean is −0.73%. Most developed-country net specifications are weak, although exceptions exist. Broader cross-sections support stronger gross results; the broad-panel headline cannot be transferred directly to a major currency pair (Tables A.12–A.14, pp. 84–85).
- **Continuation eventually reverses.** Fixed cohorts' cumulative returns peak around eight to twelve months before reversal, consistent with underreaction followed by overreaction without identifying that mechanism causally. Rolling net performance includes extended losing intervals. Tested conventional risk factors explain little short-horizon momentum, but longer formation has FX-volatility exposure, and higher idiosyncratic volatility and country risk are associated with stronger returns. Unexplained returns are not riskless (pp. 28, 32–37; Figures 4 and 7, pp. 67, 70).
- **Robustness is useful, with boundaries.** Capital-openness and tradability filters, alternative numeraires and currency-regime controls generally preserve gross momentum. Adaptive selection among 144 formation/holding strategies uses lagged information, but operates inside the original historical panel; it is not an untouched final holdout (pp. 19–20, 38–43; embedded appendix).

**Direct implication for the lab.** Keep a simple, pre-specified EUR/GBP trend benchmark separate from policy divergence and carry. Cross-sectional winner-minus-loser evidence does not establish single-pair time-series profitability. Compare benchmarks through the same forward valuation, lagged risk sizing and execution assumptions, reporting gross/net returns, turnover and cost sensitivity. The paper's monthly maturity-roll costs do not implement D028's weekly reset and residual-contract marking.

**Limitations and implementation status.** The extended copy contains more analysis than the published article. The changing universe, indicative spreads, overlapping holding-period returns and historically selected specifications constrain interpretation; the authors use dependence-aware inference, not independent monthly observations throughout. Data end in 2010. This supports benchmark and implementation diagnostics, not selection of the best historical horizon or promotion into the primary basket. D015's separation of policy signals from trend/carry benchmarks is unchanged.

## 3. Common Risk Factors in Currency Markets

**Authors and edition:** Hanno Lustig, Nick Roussanov and Adrien Verdelhan; April 2009 working manuscript, first version September 2007. **Local file:** `common risk factors in currency markets (lustig 2009).pdf`. **Read:** all 49 pages, including the appended 14 tables and three figures. The separate online supplementary appendix is not embedded. [Institutional copy of the April 2009 manuscript](https://rodneywhitecenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/0908.pdf).

**Question.** Can common currency risks explain why high-interest currencies earn more than low-interest currencies, and what properties must an international pricing model possess to reproduce that pattern?

**Data and method.** Month-end spot and one-month forward quotes run from November 1983 to March 2008. The broad sample contains 37 currencies over time, with 9 initially and at most 34 simultaneously; a developed-country subset contains 15. Each month, forward discounts rank currencies into six broad-universe portfolios; developed-only tables use five. Quotes are foreign-currency units per USD, so a foreign-currency long earns approximately forward discount minus subsequent log depreciation. Bid/ask quotes price long and short positions separately. Summary statistics use log returns, while pricing tests use returns in levels; their reported means should not be interchanged (pp. 7–13).

**Findings and important details.**

- **Carry survives the paper's costs, but costs are large.** Buying the highest-interest portfolio and selling the lowest earns 8.83% annualised gross and 4.83% net, with net Sharpe 0.54. The developed-only net spread is 3.46%, with Sharpe 0.40. These are portfolio excess-return statistics, not returns on posted capital or a bilateral EUR/GBP strategy. Forward and spot spreads are charged each month, unlike the momentum paper's retained-membership spot-cost convention. Indicative spreads are assumed conservative relative to effective execution costs (pp. 7–9; Table 1, p. 34).
- **Two common factors have different roles.** The first principal component explains about 70% of portfolio-return variation and resembles **RX**, the equal-weight average of currency-portfolio returns against USD. The second explains about 12% and resembles **HML_FX**, highest-interest minus lowest-interest returns. RX captures the broad level; HML_FX captures the slope across interest-rate portfolios. This structure is also present in spot movements, rather than merely common interest-rate variation (pp. 10–11; Table 2, p. 35).
- **Exposure lines up with average returns.** HML_FX betas rise from −0.39 to +0.61 across the six portfolios, while RX betas remain near one. The two-factor pricing fit explains about 69% of cross-sectional mean-return variation, distinct from the PCA's time-series variance result. The estimated carry risk price is 5.46% annually, versus the factor's 5.37% mean in levels. Estimated prices are uncertain, especially in the developed subset (Table 3, p. 36).
- **Stress exposes risks hidden by average correlations.** Carry's correlation with US equities reaches 0.78 during July 2007–March 2008. High-interest currencies perform poorly when independently measured foreign-equity volatility rises; low-interest currencies offer the opposite exposure. Volatility innovations support the global-risk interpretation but do not replace HML_FX in the authors' joint test. These are explanatory contemporaneous relationships, not forecasts or a complete crisis sample (pp. 22–25; Table 8, p. 41).
- **The model needs heterogeneous exposure to global risk.** Within the authors' affine, complete-market framework, country-specific heterogeneity can reproduce negative UIP regression slopes, but cannot alone explain the cross-section of carry premiums. A common shock with different country loadings can do both. Sorting by interest rates then sorts global-risk exposure, with stronger differentiation in bad times. The calibrated carry mean is a target, not independent validation of the mechanism (pp. 16–22).
- **The authors address mechanical factor fit.** Lagged 36-month beta sorts and factors built from currencies outside the test portfolios support common risk beyond the original interest-rate sort. Splitting currencies is not a chronological holdout. The momentum exercise uses a carry component extracted jointly from twelve carry/momentum portfolios, which differs from the conventional carry spread tested in the preceding paper; their explanatory-power claims are therefore not directly interchangeable (pp. 25–30; Tables 9–14, pp. 42–47).

**Direct implication for the lab.** Relative-policy repricing and the level of forward carry answer different questions. The expression comparison should investigate whether policy-conditioned returns also load on broad carry, equities or volatility, including stress periods and separately reported carry contributions. Use aligned, appropriately denominated returns; the USD RX factor cannot be imported unchanged as a GBP benchmark. Contemporaneous explanatory controls do not become available-before-entry trading signals. Common volatility sizing does not remove common-factor exposure.

**Limitations and implementation status.** Factor construction imposes some relations mechanically; non-rejection of pricing errors does not prove a unique risk explanation. Model assumptions, changing coverage, indicative costs and the March 2008 endpoint limit transfer to modern EUR/GBP. Requiring both current and subsequent quotes for sample inclusion is an ex-post availability filter to audit before replication. The findings motivate global-risk diagnostics, not a new hedge, signal or basket component. D015 and the existing instrument/basket approval gates remain unchanged.

## 4. Bond Risk Premia

**Authors and edition:** John H. Cochrane and Monika Piazzesi; *American Economic Review* 95(1), March 2005, 138–160. **Local file:** `bond risk premia (cochrane and piazzesi 2005).pdf`. **Read:** all 23 pages, seven tables, seven figures and sixteen numbered equations. The online appendix is separately published, not embedded. [Publisher record and separate appendix](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0002828053828581).

**Question.** How much does today's yield curve reveal about future bond excess returns, and can one common predictor capture that information across maturities?

**Data and method.** Monthly Fama–Bliss/CRSP US zero-coupon prices, 1964–2003, supply 1Y–5Y yields and annual forward rates. The outcomes are overlapping **one-year holding-period excess returns on initially 2Y–5Y bonds**, financed at the entry-date 1Y yield. With `p` denoting log price per unit face value and `y_t^(1)` the continuously compounded one-year yield, the decimal log excess return is:

```text
rx_(t+1)^(n) = p_(t+1)^(n−1) − p_t^(n) − y_t^(1)
```

Here `t+1` means one year later; the bond has aged by one year. For project use, this interpolated-price construction is a **modelled synthetic return**, with USD as the paper's numeraire. The authors regress average excess returns on the initial 1Y yield and four annual forward rates, then regress each maturity's return on that same fitted predictor. Average maturity loading is normalised to one. These forwards are future one-year **interest rates**, not FX forward quotes (pp. 3–6).

**Findings and important details.**

- **A common tent-shaped predictor dominates.** The fitted combination places positive weight on intermediate forward rates and negative weight at the ends. Maturity loadings rise from 0.47 for 2Y to 1.43 for 5Y bonds. Current-curve regressions explain about 35% of average-return variation and 31–37% for individual maturities. A higher score predicts higher long-bond excess returns. This rejects constant expected excess returns in the sample; the predictor is a return-forecasting state variable, not itself a traded payoff or a pure measure of policy expectations (Figure 1, p. 3; Table 1, p. 6).
- **The 44% headline requires lags.** Adding the current curve and three monthly lags raises average-return R² to 44%. A one-month lag alone retains about 35%, reducing the shared-price measurement-error problem. The authors interpret lag averaging as recovering a persistent premium obscured by noisy prices, rather than demonstrating that measurement error is absent (pp. 13–17; Table 5, p. 15).
- **Good yield fit does not guarantee good return forecasts.** Level, slope and curvature explain 99.97% of yield variance but produce only about 26% return-forecast R², versus 35% using all five yields. A small 4Y–5Y yield component contains useful return information. The tent in forward-rate weights is not simply the usual curvature factor in yields; the predictor can correlate with slope while containing important additional information (pp. 9–12; Table 4, p. 10).
- **Approximation and statistical truth differ.** The exact single-factor restriction is decisively rejected with current predictors. Nevertheless, its dominant component accounts for 99.51% of variation in fitted expected returns, not 99.51% of realised returns. Additional components forecast small maturity-specific discrepancies, and lagging weakens the rejection. The authors favour measurement error as an explanation but cannot establish whether those discrepancies survive trading costs (pp. 19–22).
- **Inference needs dependence-aware checks.** Monthly observations of annual returns overlap. The authors compare Hansen–Hodrick, Newey–West, nonoverlapping estimates and small-sample bootstraps, including an expectations-hypothesis null. Some joint statistics are numerically unstable; the main predictability survives alternative calculations, but the average-return R²'s bootstrap interval is wide, at 22–56%. Ordinary independent-observation inference would overstate confidence (pp. 6–8).
- **The economic interpretation remains incomplete.** The predictor is high around business-cycle troughs and also forecasts stock excess returns, with R² rising from 7% to 12% with lags. An affine pricing model can reproduce the annual return forecasts, but does not identify the underlying macroeconomic cause. A monthly first-order VAR misses important annual dynamics; annual findings should not be assumed to apply to weekly trades (pp. 8–9, 12–18, 23).

**Direct implication for the lab.** Curve information mixes expected policy and compensation for risk. Keep that interpretation separate from the primary policy-divergence signal, and preserve price, carry/roll and financing components when constructing rates P&L. Any diagnostic replication must estimate coefficients using only fully realised training outcomes and separate overlapping training/test targets. A US maturity loading is not a UK–Germany DV01 hedge ratio; evaluate country legs and the relative exposure directly.

**Limitations and implementation status.** The paper asserts real-time, subsample, trading-rule and alternative-data robustness, but their detailed evidence is in the absent appendix. This local article provides no net, risk-normalised ranking, Sharpe comparison or approved 2s10s direction; interpolated zero-coupon prices are not execution records. US 2Y–5Y annual evidence does not validate UK/EA OIS par-rate or 10Y signals. D010/D011 rates and curve gates remain OPEN; no primary signal or frozen convention changes.

## 5. The Probability of Backtest Overfitting

**Authors and edition:** David H. Bailey, Jonathan M. Borwein, Marcos López de Prado and Qiji Jim Zhu; revised manuscript dated 27 February 2015. **Local file:** `probability of backtest overfitting (bailey 2015).pdf`. **Read:** all 34 pages, thirteen figures and four numbered equations; no embedded appendix. Detailed simulation validation and implementation code are delegated to separately referenced papers. [Author-hosted manuscript](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf).

**Question.** When researchers choose the best historical result from many configurations, how can they assess whether that selection process extracts a persistent signal or rewards sample-specific noise?

**Data and method.** The proposal works on a matrix of already computed performance series: common dates in rows and `N` tested configurations in columns. **Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV)** divides dates into an even number `S` of equal contiguous blocks. For every choice of half the blocks, it selects the best configuration in-sample (IS), then ranks that same configuration among all candidates on the complementary out-of-sample (OOS) blocks. With OOS rank `r` increasing from worst to best:

```text
relative_rank = r / (N + 1)
logit         = log(relative_rank / (1 − relative_rank))
```

Negative logits indicate below-median OOS ranking. Their frequency estimates **PBO**, conditional on the candidate family, metric and resampling scheme. IS here concerns configuration selection, not training the underlying model: CSCV does not reconstruct or validate that model's information set (pp. 9–13).

**Findings and important details.**

- **Selection changes the statistical question.** A nominal 5% false-positive rate for one test does not control error after searching many configurations on the same data. The chosen Sharpe ratio incorporates the search process. Neither a high historical Sharpe nor conventional single-strategy significance accounts for that search automatically (pp. 3–7).
- **Four diagnostics answer different questions.** PBO measures relative ranking failure; performance degradation compares selected IS and OOS scores; probability of loss measures negative selected OOS performance; stochastic dominance compares selected outcomes with the overall candidate family's distribution. Low PBO can coexist with losses, while high PBO can arise among similarly skilled strategies. It is not the probability that a strategy is unprofitable, a conventional p-value, or proof that every candidate lacks skill (pp. 13–20, 24).
- **Similar impressive backtests can conceal different evidence.** A seasonal strategy optimised on 1,000 random-walk daily observations achieves Sharpe 1.27, yet has PBO about 55% and negative OOS Sharpe in about 53% of splits. Adding an artificial monthly signal produces a similar headline Sharpe, 1.54, with PBO about 13%. These are illustrative simulations demonstrating selection risk, not universal performance thresholds (pp. 25–33).
- **A complete, honest candidate family is essential.** Hiding unsuccessful trials changes the ranking benchmark; adding deliberately doomed alternatives can also manipulate it. For guided optimisation, the authors recommend treating completed search outcomes as configurations rather than treating every intermediate step as an independent strategy. The intended search process and eligible candidates must therefore be documented explicitly (pp. 24–25).
- **CSCV has temporal and statistical limits.** Equal sample sizes and complementary splits make comparisons reproducible, but OOS blocks can precede IS blocks. Contiguous blocks preserve some dependence within blocks, not all dependence across their boundaries. The many combinations reuse the same observations and are not independent datasets. Strong autocorrelation, small candidate sets, block length and unseen regime changes constrain interpretation (pp. 20–24).
- **The diagnostic must not become the optimisation target.** The authors explicitly warn against searching for configurations that minimise PBO. Their suggested 5% rejection threshold is a proposal, not a universally calibrated guarantee. CSCV cannot repair lookahead, incorrect costs or flawed P&L; a reliable selection diagnostic still needs reliable backtests (pp. 14, 24–25).

**Direct implication for the lab.** Use the existing [Experiment Register](../project/EXPERIMENTS.md) to record every material attempt, including unattractive results: signal horizons, transformations, execution timing and instrument maintenance rules, risk estimates, costs, regimes and basket variants. Keep the economically justified family small. If CSCV is used diagnostically, build aligned, point-in-time, net performance columns on the common risk basis and state the selection metric in advance. Retain chronological walk-forward evaluation, separation of overlapping outcomes, and the untouched final holdout. D014's date-selection gate and D019's freeze-before-holdout requirement remain controlling.

**Limitations and implementation status.** This manuscript contains inconsistencies: the main definition uses the median while the conclusion says mean; finite-rank boundary conventions differ; and `S = 16` yields **12,870** splits, despite the printed 12,780. Any implementation needs explicit, consistent tie/median rules. The detailed Monte Carlo/extreme-value validation is outside this PDF. No CSCV implementation, block count, threshold, sample split or risk configuration is approved here; this note strengthens existing research discipline without changing decisions.
